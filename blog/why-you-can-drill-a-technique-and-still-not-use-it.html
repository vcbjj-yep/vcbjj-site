Still Rolling — Learning

Why You Can Drill a Technique and Still Not Use It

Coach Vince VCBJJ, Bangsar

You drilled the escape eleven times on Tuesday. Your partner was patient. The coach nodded. You walked out feeling like you owned it.

On Thursday you get mounted in the first round and do none of it. You lie there. The escape arrives about forty seconds after the bell, like a guest who read the wrong date on the invitation.

That is the specific embarrassment. Not the loss. The memory showing up late, in perfect detail, when it no longer helps.

A question keeps turning up in beginner threads online: why can’t I remember anything when I roll? People ask it as if something is wrong with their memory. Nothing is wrong with your memory.

What the drill actually trained

Count what a drill gives you. Your partner stands still or moves on a script. You know the starting position. You know which technique is coming because the coach just showed it. Nobody is trying to stop you.

Now count what rolling gives you. Nobody announces the position. Your partner is working against you. You have about two seconds to notice where you are, pick a response, and start it.

A drill trains the last part of that sequence, the movement. Rolling asks for three parts. The first two, noticing and choosing, got no reps at all. You practiced answering a question you had already been told.

Practice carries over best to situations that look like the practice. A drill that removes the noticing and the choosing will not give you the noticing and the choosing later.

What it costs you

The cost is not one lost round. The cost is the story you build around it. After enough Thursdays, you decide you are the kind of person who can’t retain this stuff. Adults who start late accept that story fast, because it looks like the obvious explanation.

It is the wrong explanation. The drilling was fine. The practice left out the hard part.

What I believed, and what I know now

When I started coaching, I thought the fix was more reps. Drill it thirty times instead of ten. What I learned is that reps of the same kind produce the same result. Thirty cooperative reps build a very reliable cooperative technique.

Three changes that cost nothing

Finish every drill with a live version. Same starting position, same single goal, but your partner now resists. Start light. Your coach can set the level. The point is that you have to notice the position and decide to start, which is the part the drill skipped.

Write three lines after class. What you tried. What stopped you. What you will try next time. A note on your phone works. It takes two minutes, and it turns a vague feeling of having trained into something you can check.

Recall before you read. Before the next class, cover the note and say out loud what you planned to try. Then check. Pulling something out of memory strengthens it more than looking at it again, which is why watching the video a second time does less than you hope.

The note, in full:
Tried: ______
Stopped by: ______
Next time: ______

I wrote about the nervous system side of this problem in Why Your Brain Freezes During Rolling. This post covers the practice side. Both exist, and they need different fixes, so check which one you have before you change anything.

I still have no clean answer for the student who arrives after a bad week at work with nothing left. The rule says start light. The student says start at zero. I have not settled that, and I do not think a rule will.
